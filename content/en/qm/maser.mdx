## The ammonia maser

Ammonia, NH₃, is a pyramid: three hydrogens form a triangular base, and the nitrogen sits on one side of their plane or the other. The two arrangements are mirror images of each other, and classically both are equally good equilibria. Call them $|L\rangle$ and $|R\rangle$, for the nitrogen to the left or to the right of the plane. Quantum mechanically the nitrogen can tunnel from one side to the other, and what follows from that one fact is close to the simplest nontrivial system in quantum mechanics: two states, coupled by a single number. It is also the system that gave masers, and shortly after lasers, their name.

### A double well

Start from the nitrogen itself. Let $x$ be its position along the molecule's axis, with the plane of the hydrogens at $x = 0$. Its energy is lowest in either pyramid and highest in the plane between them, a symmetric double well, modelled here as $V(x) = x^4 - 4x^2$: two minima at $x = \pm\sqrt2$, separated by a barrier of height 4 in units where $\hbar = m = 1$. Solving the Schrödinger equation in it gives the stationary states, and the lowest two come as a close pair: a symmetric $\psi_S$ and an antisymmetric $\psi_A$, each shared equally between the wells, at energies $E_S < E_A$ only a small gap apart, a gap the next sections name $2\Delta$. Neither puts the nitrogen on one side. Their sum and difference do,
$$
\psi_R = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(\psi_S + \psi_A\big), \qquad \psi_L = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(\psi_S - \psi_A\big),
$$
since the two eigenstates reinforce in one well and cancel in the other. These are the two configurations as wavefunctions, $\psi_L = \langle x|L\rangle$ and $\psi_R = \langle x|R\rangle$. The states one would naively start from are not stationary: they are superpositions of the ones that are.

The third level, $E_3$, is what lets everything else be dropped. It lies above the barrier and far from the pair, $E_3 - E_A$ being over twenty times $E_A - E_S$, so at energies near the doublet the rest of the spectrum plays no part. Two states are all there is, and the rest of this page works with just those.

### Two configurations, one Hamiltonian

Restrict attention to $|L\rangle$ and $|R\rangle$ and write the Hamiltonian in that basis. Left alone in either well, the molecule would sit at the same energy $E_0$, since the two configurations are mirror images and nothing distinguishes them energetically. That fixes the diagonal:
$$
\langle L|\hat H|L\rangle = \langle R|\hat H|R\rangle = E_0.
$$
The barrier separating the wells is finite, though, so the nitrogen has a small but nonzero amplitude to tunnel through it rather than stay put. That coupling is the off-diagonal element, real by the same mirror symmetry, written $-\Delta$:
$$
\langle L|\hat H|R\rangle = \langle R|\hat H|L\rangle = -\Delta, \qquad \hat H = \begin{pmatrix} E_0 & -\Delta \\ -\Delta & E_0 \end{pmatrix}.
$$
$\Delta > 0$ measures how easily the molecule tunnels: a taller or wider barrier makes $\Delta$ smaller, and $\Delta = 0$ decouples the wells entirely, leaving $|L\rangle$ and $|R\rangle$ each stationary on its own.

### Symmetric and antisymmetric states

$\hat H$ is not diagonal in $\{|L\rangle, |R\rangle\}$, so those are not the states of definite energy. Diagonalising it gives two,
$$
|S\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|L\rangle + |R\rangle\big), \quad E_S = E_0 - \Delta;
\qquad
|A\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|R\rangle - |L\rangle\big), \quad E_A = E_0 + \Delta.
$$
These are the $\psi_S$ and $\psi_A$ of the double well, recovered from two numbers rather than a differential equation. The symmetric state lies *lower*, since the coupling enters as $-\Delta$. The overall sign of $|A\rangle$ is a convention, chosen to match $\psi_A$, positive in the right well.

Tunneling, invisible on the diagonal, has split a single energy $E_0$ into two, $2\Delta$ apart. The symmetric combination sits lower and the antisymmetric one higher, the same pattern that puts a bonding orbital below an antibonding one. For real ammonia $2\Delta/h \approx 23.87\ \text{GHz}$, a wavelength of about $1.25\ \text{cm}$: a photon at that frequency connects $|S\rangle$ and $|A\rangle$. Cleeton and Williams measured this absorption line in 1934, and it is exactly the transition that Gordon, Zeiger and Townes drove into oscillation around 1953–54 to build the first maser, *Microwave Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation*, the direct ancestor of the laser.

### The molecule flips

Prepare the molecule in a definite configuration, $|\psi(0)\rangle = |R\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|S\rangle + |A\rangle\big)$: an equal superposition, not an eigenstate, so it does not sit still. Each component turns at its own rate, set by $E_S$ and $E_A$,
$$
|\psi(t)\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Big(e^{-iE_S t/\hbar}|S\rangle + e^{-iE_A t/\hbar}|A\rangle\Big),
$$
and projecting back onto the original basis gives
$$
P_R(t) = |\langle R|\psi(t)\rangle|^2 = \cos^2\!\Big(\frac{\Delta t}{\hbar}\Big),
\qquad
P_L(t) = |\langle L|\psi(t)\rangle|^2 = \sin^2\!\Big(\frac{\Delta t}{\hbar}\Big).
$$
The nitrogen genuinely oscillates from one side of the molecule to the other, at angular frequency $2\Delta/\hbar$, exactly the gap that separates $E_S$ from $E_A$. Ask a different question, though: not *where* the nitrogen is, but *what energy a measurement would return*. Nothing moves at all. $|c_S(t)|^2 = |c_A(t)|^2 = \tfrac12$ for every $t$, since $|S\rangle$ and $|A\rangle$ are themselves stationary states and only their phase evolves. The flipping lives entirely in the configuration basis $\{|L\rangle, |R\rangle\}$; it is invisible to a measurement made in the energy basis.

### From oscillation to amplification

A beam of ammonia by itself just flips back and forth. Turning that into a maser needs one more piece, a way to separate $|S\rangle$ from $|A\rangle$ before they reach a cavity. Each geometric configuration carries an electric dipole moment, pointing opposite ways in $|L\rangle$ and $|R\rangle$, so an external electric field pushes the two levels apart in energy by an amount that grows with the field and has opposite sign for each. Passing the molecular beam through a strongly inhomogeneous field turns that state-dependent Stark shift into a spatial sort: the upper state $|A\rangle$ is focused into a beam, $|S\rangle$ is steered away. What enters the cavity next is a population living almost entirely in $|A\rangle$, a population inversion, upside down from thermal equilibrium, feeding a cavity resonant at $2\Delta/\hbar$. A single photon at that frequency, striking one of those molecules, stimulates the $|A\rangle \to |S\rangle$ transition and comes out accompanied by a second photon identical to the first. Repeated across the whole beam, that is amplification by stimulated emission, the physics behind the name.
